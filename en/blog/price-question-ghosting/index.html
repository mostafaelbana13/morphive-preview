The customer asks "how much?" and disappears. What's going wrong?

The most common message any business gets, and the conversation most likely to die. Why customers vanish after hearing the price, and how to reply so the conversation stays alive.

If you gathered every message your business received last month, the most common one is probably two words: “how much?”. And if you followed those conversations, you’d find a big share of them ended after one reply: the price, then silence.

Business owners usually explain this as “the customer found it expensive”. Sometimes that’s true. But often the problem isn’t the number itself. It’s the way the number arrives.

The price question isn’t only about price

When a customer types “how much?”, what they are really asking is “is it worth it?”. Price is a number; value is a judgment. And nobody can judge a number on its own.

Imagine you ask about a training course and the reply is: “2,500 EGP.” You don’t know how many hours, who teaches it, or what you’ll learn. The easiest move is to close the chat and compare.

Why customers disappear

1. The number arrived alone

A price without context gets compared to the cheapest alternative the customer knows, even if that alternative is far worse.

2. They don’t know what makes you different

If you don’t say why you’re different, the customer assumes you’re like everyone else, and then the cheapest option is the logical one. This is what I call the one advantage that sets you apart: one real thing the customer can verify.

3. You didn’t ask for a next step

A reply that ends with the number leaves the ball in the customer’s court, and the customer is busy. A reply that ends with a simple question keeps the conversation open.

4. The reply came late

A few hours later, the customer has asked three other businesses. Whoever replied first, and clearly, usually made the sale.

A weak reply and a strong one

Weak:

It’s 2,500 EGP.

Strong:

Hi, thanks for asking. The course is 8 live sessions with hands-on work on your own project, plus a month of follow-up after it ends. The price is 2,500 EGP. Would you like me to send you the full session outline?

Same number, but it arrives after the customer knows what they get. And the reply ends with a question they can answer with a single “yes”.

Practical steps to improve your replies

  1. Pull your last twenty conversations that started with the price question, and note where each one stopped.
  2. Write three sentences explaining what the customer gets for the price. Just three, with numbers and specifics.
  3. Pick one advantage that sets you apart, and put it before the number.
  4. End every reply with one question that leads to the next step: sending details, booking a time, choosing a size.
  5. Follow up once with anyone who didn’t reply, a day later, without pushing.

Following up without pushing

A customer who didn’t reply isn’t necessarily uninterested. They may have got busy, wanted to ask someone, or simply forgotten. One follow-up message the next day brings a fair share of these conversations back.

Weak follow-up:

You didn’t reply! Are you still interested?

Better follow-up:

Good morning. I sent you the course details yesterday. If you have any questions about timing or payment, I’m here. And if you’d like, I can send a short clip from one of the sessions so you can see how it’s taught.

The second message doesn’t blame the customer, and it opens a new door: a specific question, or a sample that shows the value. One message is enough; pushing after that does more harm than good.

Should I put the price in the post?

People ask this a lot, and there’s no single answer. If your price is competitive and simple, showing it filters out casual messages. If you charge more than the market because you offer more, explain the value in the post first. I wrote about this in detail in how to write a post that sells.

Common mistakes

  • “Price in DMs” on every post: brings lots of messages, and many of them vanish.
  • Discounting the moment a customer hesitates: it teaches them the first price wasn’t real.
  • Very long messages: three clear sentences beat a full paragraph.
  • An auto-reply on its own: fine as a start, but it doesn’t answer “is it worth it?”.

The short version

  • “How much?” usually means “is it worth it?”.
  • The number comes after the value, not before it.
  • One clear advantage, then the price, then a question that leads to the next step.
  • Review your real conversations before deciding the problem is the price.

Try it on your business

Open your last ten conversations that began with the price question, and next to each write where it stopped. You’ll find a pattern, and that’s the first thing to fix.
