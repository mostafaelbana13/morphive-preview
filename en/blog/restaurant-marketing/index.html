Restaurant marketing: turning views into visits and orders

A video of your restaurant hit a hundred thousand views, and the dining room was half empty that same week. Why people watch and don't come, and what actually gets them to visit or order.

A restaurant posted a video of a new dish and it took off: a hundred thousand views in two days, and thousands of "that looks amazing" comments. Then the week went by, and the dining room looked the same. So did the orders.

The video didn't fail. It just did a different job from the one the owner wanted. This article is about the difference between content people watch and content that brings someone to your door.

Why people watch and don't come

Because many of the people watching can't come at all. Viral videos reach people in other cities, other countries, and at times they aren't thinking about food.

A restaurant is a local business. Your real customer lives within reasonable distance and gets hungry while you're open. Any content that doesn't reach that person stays pleasant entertainment.

What gets people to visit

1. Photos that genuinely make people hungry

The dish in the photo should look the way it arrives. Natural light, an angle that shows size and texture, and a little movement: sauce being poured, cheese stretching. An exaggerated photo earns one visit, then a bad review.

2. A reason to come now

"Our food is delicious" is not a reason to come today. These are:

  • A dish available this week only.
  • A weekday lunch deal.
  • A seasonal menu.

Content tied to a specific moment turns "I'll try it someday" into "let's go tonight".

3. Maps before Instagram

When someone decides where to eat right now, they usually open a map, not Instagram. That's where they see distance, rating, photos and hours. If your map profile has old photos and wrong hours, you've lost a decision that was already made.

4. Delivery apps

In Egypt and the Gulf, a large share of orders goes through delivery apps. Your page there is your restaurant's second storefront: clear photos for every item, descriptions that mention ingredients and size, and a menu that's easy to scroll.

The customer who comes back

Winning a new customer is expensive. Bringing back someone who tried you and liked it is much easier. Yet most restaurants put all their effort into new customers.

Simple ideas:

  • Know your regulars: a list of people who order often, with their permission.
  • Give them a reason to return: a new dish offered to them first, or a small surprise on the fifth visit.
  • Reply to reviews: a customer who sees you replied feels someone is listening.

A one-week plan for a restaurant

If you don't know where to start, try this week as written, then adjust to what you see:

  • Day 1: a short video of one dish from prep to plate, with its price and your location in the caption.
  • Day 2: refresh your map photos and reply to every unanswered review.
  • Day 3: a post about the weekday lunch deal, with a clear end date.
  • Day 4: a photo of someone on the team with a line about them. People like knowing who cooks for them.
  • Day 5: audit your delivery app page: an item without a photo, a thin description, an old price.
  • Day 6: content about the evening atmosphere, because weekend plans get made now.
  • Day 7: share a photo or comment from a real customer, with their permission.

At the end of the week, don't ask how many views you got. Ask how many bookings and orders came in compared with the week before.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring success in views: ask instead how many bookings or orders came in this week.
  • Content that's only about dishes: people also come for the place, the atmosphere and the service.
  • Ignoring negative reviews: an unanswered review is read by everyone considering a visit.
  • App photos that don't match reality: they lead to returned orders and weak ratings.

Common questions

Should I work with influencers? It can help, if the influencer's audience actually lives near you. Ask where their followers are before asking how many there are.

Phone or professional photographer? A phone is fine for everyday content in good light. Photos for the menu, delivery apps and maps are worth one professional shoot.

How often should I post? Consistency beats volume. A schedule you can keep is better than one enthusiastic week followed by a silent month.

The short version

  • Views aren't visits. Your customer is nearby and hungry now.
  • An honest photo, a reason to come now, an up-to-date map profile, and a tidy delivery page.
  • A returning customer costs less than a new one, so look after them.

Try it on your restaurant

Open a map now and search for your type of food near your restaurant. Do you show up? Are your photos better than your nearest competitor's? Then open your delivery app page and look at it as a hungry person in a hurry.
